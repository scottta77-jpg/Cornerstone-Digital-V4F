## Article body

January can produce a long list of marketing intentions: post more often, rebuild the website, run adverts, try AI and improve SEO. Without a clear objective, the list becomes a set of disconnected jobs competing with normal client work.

**The short answer:** choose one business objective, identify the main obstacle to it and plan a manageable set of actions over 90 days. Give each action an owner, a budget or time allowance and a way to assess progress. Review the evidence before adding more activity.

## Begin with the business result

Decide what you want the next quarter to achieve. More suitable enquiries for a particular service? More appointments from existing traffic? A more dependable pipeline for a team with spare capacity?

Be specific about the service and customer. A hypothetical consultancy might want more enquiries for a defined assessment package rather than “more engagement”. Agree how many requests the team could realistically handle.

Hallam's discussion of segmentation and targeting is a useful reminder that choosing the audience is part of strategy. A small business needs a practical focus as much as a larger organisation does. [Further reading: Hallam](https://hallam.agency/blog/revisiting-segmentation-and-targeting/).

## Establish a starting point

Review recent qualified enquiries, their sources, the services requested and what happened next. Note gaps in the information rather than inventing a precise baseline.

Look for an obvious constraint. You may have enough visitors but unclear service information, or enough enquiries but slow follow-up. These situations call for different work. Use our [lead-tracking guide](/blog/track-marketing-paying-customers/) to connect activity with customer outcomes.

## Choose a small number of priorities

Pick the actions most likely to address the constraint. Explain why each matters before committing resources. A new channel is not automatically the best next step.

For example, a business losing suitable enquiries at the booking stage could first simplify that journey, confirm availability and improve the follow-up process. Paying for more traffic before checking those steps may increase the workload without resolving the problem.

## Organise the quarter into three stages

| Stage | Main question | Example work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Days 1–30: understand and repair | Can customers take the intended next step? | Check tracking, clarify the offer and fix a broken enquiry route |
| Days 31–60: deliver a focused test | Does the chosen activity reach suitable buyers? | Publish relevant evidence or test a tightly scoped campaign |
| Days 61–90: review and improve | What should we continue, change or stop? | Compare outcomes, refine the journey and plan the next quarter |

These stages are a planning structure, not a promise that search rankings or revenue will change within a fixed period.

## Give each action a workable brief

Write down the audience, purpose, deliverable, owner and due date. Include dependencies such as approved photographs, access to the booking system or a client's permission to publish a case study.

For content, define the buying question being answered and the next step it supports. Our [case-study guide](/blog/customer-case-study-guide/) explains how completed work can become useful evidence instead of another generic article.

Keep the plan realistic alongside delivery commitments. A smaller set of completed actions is easier to evaluate than a large list of half-finished ideas.

## Set limits on spending and staff time

Agree what the business can commit, including supplier costs, advertising spend, software and internal hours. Keep a clear distinction between the cost of setup and the cost of ongoing operation.

For an illustrative test, define a maximum spend and a review point before it begins. Decide what evidence would justify continuing. Avoid treating a forecast as money already earned or using a universal percentage of turnover without checking the business's circumstances.

## Choose measures that support decisions

Use a small set of relevant outcomes: qualified enquiries, bookings attended, quotes accepted and customers acquired. Add a few supporting indicators that help explain the result, such as relevant visits or form completion.

Record what changed and when. If a new page, campaign and sales process all launch together, be careful about attributing the whole outcome to one element. Keep unknowns visible.

## Build in a short review routine

Check delivery progress regularly and review meaningful outcomes at agreed points. Ask what has been learned, what needs a decision and who will act. Avoid changing direction after every small fluctuation.

At the end of the quarter, keep the work that is useful, stop activity that no longer has a case and set the next priorities from the evidence. Include ongoing [website care](/blog/website-maintenance-after-launch/) so improvements remain dependable.
