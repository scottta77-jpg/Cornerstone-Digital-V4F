# Google Business Profile or website: what should each do for your business?

Planned Monday: 2026-10-26 | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Google Business Profile vs Website: What Each Should Do

Meta description: Understand how Google Business Profile and your website work together. Check business details, service information, contact routes and local enquiry journeys.

Card excerpt: Give your Google profile and website clear jobs, then connect them so local customers can find the right information and make contact.

A customer finds your business on Google Maps, checks the reviews and taps through to your website. The profile says you handle weekend appointments. The website says Monday to Friday. Before anyone calls, there is already a question about which information to trust.

**The short answer:** your Google Business Profile helps eligible businesses appear with useful local information; your website gives customers the detail and contact journey you control. Give each a clear role and keep shared facts consistent. Treat them as connected parts of the same customer experience.

## What should your Google profile do?

Help someone identify the right business and check the basics: what you do, where you serve, when you operate and how to contact you. Use the real business name and appropriate categories, with accurate contact details and current photographs.

Google's guidelines require businesses to represent themselves accurately and use a precise address or service area. Eligibility depends on the business model; a profile is not automatically available to every online-only operation. [Source: Google Business Profile guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en).

A profile can answer an immediate question, such as whether you are open. It has less room to explain a complicated service or help someone compare several project options.

## What should the website do?

Give the customer enough detail to make the next decision. A service page can explain the work included, relevant experience, process, likely requirements and what happens after an enquiry.

For a hypothetical landscaping business, the profile might establish the business and operating area. The website can distinguish a garden redesign from routine maintenance, explain the site-visit process and show genuine examples of each type of work.

That distinction helps people ask for the right service. It also gives you a useful destination when someone needs more than an opening time or phone number.

## Decide which information belongs where

| Customer question | Profile's role | Website's role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is this the right business? | Accurate name, category and contact details | Clear identity, service descriptions and genuine work |
| Do they cover my area? | Accurate location or service area | Practical coverage, exclusions and travel considerations |
| What does this service involve? | A concise overview | A fuller explanation of scope and process |
| How do I take the next step? | Relevant contact or website action | A working enquiry, booking or purchase journey |

You do not need identical wording everywhere. You do need the underlying facts to agree.

## Follow the journey from the profile

Open your profile as a customer and follow its website link. Is the destination useful? Can someone recognise the business, find the service they came for and make contact on a phone?

A general homepage may work for a broad business introduction. Where a specific action or service link is available, use a destination that matches it. Check the final page and form, not just whether the URL opens.

Then try the phone route. A tap on a number is a different outcome from a call being answered. Make sure your team knows what happens when a call arrives during a visit or outside normal hours.

## Keep changes connected

Write a short update checklist for changes to your phone number, services, opening hours or coverage. Assign someone to update the website and relevant profiles together.

Consider a change to Saturday appointments. The website, booking calendar, profile and phone message may all need attention. Updating only one creates conflicting expectations. Give temporary information a review date so it does not become permanent by accident.

## Measure outcomes from both places

Review profile activity alongside website enquiries and confirmed customer outcomes. Different reporting tools may count different actions, so define what each number means before combining them.

Where appropriate, a tagged website link can help identify profile visits in analytics. It will not reveal every part of a customer's research. Ask how the customer found you and record a useful answer alongside the enquiry.

Our [local SEO and AI search guide](/blog/local-seo-ai-search-small-business/) explains the broader visibility foundations. For the customer feedback side, read [how to ask for Google reviews](/blog/ask-for-google-reviews/).

## Make one complete route dependable

Start with the service you most want to sell. Check its profile information, website explanation and contact route from beginning to end. Fix conflicting facts and unnecessary steps before adding more content.

# Landing page vs service page: where should you send your ad traffic?

Planned Monday: 2026-11-02 | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Landing Page vs Service Page: Where Should Ads Lead?

Meta description: Compare landing pages and service pages for paid advertising. Learn when to use each, what to include and how to judge the quality of the enquiries.

Card excerpt: Choose the right destination for paid-ad visitors by matching the offer, customer questions and next step to the campaign.

Someone clicks an advert for a boiler service and arrives on a homepage covering installations, plumbing, bathrooms and general maintenance. The business may offer exactly what they need, but the visitor has to find it again.

**The short answer:** send ad visitors to the page that best continues the promise they clicked. A focused campaign landing page can suit one offer and audience. A well-built service page can work when it already answers the relevant questions and provides a clear next step.

## Start with the advert's promise

Write down what the advert offers, who it is intended for and what you want the person to do. Then compare that with the destination page. The wording need not match word for word, but the offer, eligibility and action should agree.

A campaign advertising an initial consultation needs a page explaining that consultation. A page describing every service in the company may make the customer do unnecessary work. Equally, a separate page adds little if the existing service page already handles the task well.

## What is a campaign landing page?

For this comparison, it is a page designed around a particular campaign, audience and action. Technically, any page can be a visitor's landing page; the useful distinction is the purpose behind it.

A campaign page might explain a specific assessment, event or seasonal service. Its content, proof and form can be selected for that decision. Exposure Ninja's landing-page guide also stresses the importance of delivering what an advert promises. [Further reading: Exposure Ninja](https://exposureninja.com/blog/how-to-create-a-high-converting-landing-page/).

## When can a service page do the job?

Use the existing service page when it closely matches the campaign and gives customers the information they need. It should explain the offer, provide relevant evidence, handle common concerns and make contact straightforward.

Check the mobile experience and enquiry destination. A strong service page may be a better starting point than a hurried duplicate created only because someone said every campaign needs a new landing page.

## Compare the requirements

| Situation | Useful starting point |
| --- | --- |
| Advertising one established service to the same customers the page already serves | Review the existing service page |
| Promoting a specific event, assessment or limited offer | Consider a focused campaign page |
| Speaking to a distinct customer group with different questions | Adapt the experience around those differences |
| Testing a new proposition | Use a controlled page version and a clear measurement plan |

Choose around the customer decision and the work required to support it.

## What should the page contain?

Make the offer clear in the opening section. Explain who it suits, what the customer receives and any important exclusions. Put genuine evidence near the claims it supports.

Use a next step that fits the purchase. Requesting a site visit is different from buying a fixed service. Tell the customer what information you need and what happens after they submit it.

Keep practical routes to business identity, contact details and privacy information. A focused page should still let someone investigate the business and understand the terms of the offer.

## Match the form to the action

If you are offering a callback, collect enough information to make a useful call. If a postcode determines whether you can serve the customer, explain why it is needed. Save detailed project questions for the appropriate stage.

Test confirmation messages, delivery to the team and any acknowledgement. An attractive campaign page is not useful if its enquiries go to an unattended inbox. Assign an owner before paying for traffic.

## Judge qualified enquiries, not just submissions

Agree what makes an enquiry suitable. That might involve service, location, timeframe or an achievable project scope. Track which submissions become meaningful conversations and customers.

A hypothetical page generating 20 forms with two suitable enquiries is not automatically better than one generating ten forms with six suitable enquiries. Those numbers illustrate the comparison; they are not a benchmark or prediction.

Avoid changing the page, targeting and offer simultaneously if you want to understand which change helped. Record the dates and allow a sensible amount of data to accumulate.

## Give the page a clear brief

Before commissioning anything, define the audience, offer, evidence, required action and measurement. # What should your contact form ask? A practical guide to better enquiries

Planned Monday: 2026-11-09 | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: What Should a Contact Form Ask? A Practical Guide

Meta description: Choose useful contact-form questions for your business. Balance lead quality, customer effort, accessibility, privacy and reliable enquiry delivery.

Card excerpt: Choose form questions that help your team respond usefully, while making the first enquiry straightforward for the customer.

A contact form is often asked to do too much. It tries to gather a full project brief, qualify the budget, collect marketing preferences and start a sale before the customer has spoken to anyone.

**The short answer:** ask for the information needed to route the enquiry and give a useful first response. Make the purpose of each question clear. Add more detail when it genuinely changes the next step, and move the rest to a later conversation.

## Decide what happens after submission

Start with the team's job. Does someone need to arrange a callback, confirm coverage, prepare a quote or send the enquiry to a specialist? The form should support that task.

A hypothetical plumbing business may need the type of work, postcode and a contact method. A design consultancy may need the service requested, a short brief and a preferred way to respond. Their first forms should not be identical simply because both need leads.

## Choose a sensible starting set

| Question | When it helps | What to consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | Personalising and identifying the enquiry | Ask for the level of detail you need |
| Email or phone | Making the requested reply | Explain if a particular method is essential |
| Service required | Routing to the right team | Use customer-friendly choices |
| Location or postcode | Confirming service coverage | Collect a full address later if unnecessary now |
| Short description | Understanding the request | Give a helpful example or prompt |
| Timeframe | Prioritising realistic next steps | Include an option for people who are unsure |

This is a starting point for discussion, not a requirement to add every field.

## Should budget be compulsory?

Ask whether the answer changes the first conversation. A consultancy dealing with a substantial minimum scope may need to explain its starting point. A repair business may not expect a customer to know the likely cost before diagnosis.

If budget is useful, explain what the question helps you do and offer sensible ranges or an “I'm not sure” option. Avoid using an arbitrary number to reject people before understanding the request.

## Should you ask for file uploads?

A photograph can help explain a damaged part or a reference design. Uploads also create requirements for file handling, access, storage and customer support. Make sure the business can use them responsibly.

If images are unnecessary for the first reply, offer a way to provide them later. A customer should not need to search their phone for documents just to ask whether you offer a service.

## Make the form understandable

Use visible labels, clear required-field indicators and useful error messages. Explain what went wrong and how to correct it. Keep previously entered information where practical.

Check the form with a keyboard, on a phone and with the page enlarged. Placeholder text is not a substitute for a persistent label. [Reference: W3C WAI form instructions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/instructions/).

Our [accessibility checklist](/blog/website-accessibility-checklist-small-business/) gives a broader starting point for checking the customer journey.

## Collect information for a defined purpose

Keep each field connected to a genuine need. The ICO's data-minimisation guidance explains that personal information should be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for its purpose. [Source: ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-protection-principles/a-guide-to-the-data-protection-principles/data-minimisation/).

Provide appropriate privacy information and handle marketing preferences separately from the enquiry itself. Decide who can access submissions and how long the business needs them.

## Make the response dependable

Submit test enquiries and check the customer's confirmation, the team's inbox and any connected records. Include missing optional fields, a mistyped address, duplicate submissions and an unavailable destination service.

Give the enquiry an owner. If an acknowledgement promises a response time, make sure the team can meet it. A successful on-screen message should not be the end of the process.

## Review useful outcomes

Measure completed forms, suitable enquiries and the work your team does to clarify missing information. If customers abandon the form, investigate the task before simply deleting every qualification question.

# Which marketing brings paying customers? A simple lead-tracking system

Planned Monday: 2026-11-16 | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: How to Track Which Marketing Brings Paying Customers

Meta description: Find out which marketing produces useful enquiries and customers. Build a simple tracking process across campaigns, website forms and sales outcomes.

Card excerpt: Connect website activity with enquiries, quotes and customers using a simple record your team can maintain.

Your website report shows more visits. Your social account shows more reach. Your advertising report shows more conversions. Yet when you ask which activity brought this month's customers, nobody can give a dependable answer.

**The short answer:** connect the source of an enquiry with its later outcome. Use consistent definitions, collect the relevant information at entry and keep the record updated as the conversation progresses. A basic process used reliably can be more useful than a complicated dashboard nobody maintains.

## Agree what counts as a useful outcome

Separate a website action from a qualified enquiry and a paying customer. A click on the phone number shows interest. A connected call may be a new lead, an existing customer or a wrong number.

Define qualification around your business: the right service, a workable location and a request you can realistically fulfil. Give the team the same definitions so the figures mean the same thing every month.

## Start with a small enquiry record

For a hypothetical local installation company, a simple record might contain:

| Field | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Enquiry reference and date | Connects follow-up activity to the original request |
| Service and area | Helps assess suitability |
| Source and campaign, where known | Shows the route into the business |
| Assigned owner and next action | Makes the conversation actionable |
| Stage | Distinguishes enquiry, quote, won and lost |
| Outcome date and reason | Helps explain what happened |
| Relevant order value | Supports later commercial reporting |

Keep personal contact details in the appropriate controlled business system. Avoid collecting additional information simply because a report might be interesting one day.

## Capture campaign information consistently

Use consistent campaign names and appropriate tracking links where they help. Google's URL-builder guidance explains how UTM parameters identify referral campaigns in Analytics. [Source: Google Analytics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10917952?hl=en).

For example, an email newsletter link could identify the source, medium and campaign. Do not use campaign tags on ordinary internal navigation to try to create a new acquisition source. Keep a simple naming convention so one campaign does not appear under several spellings.

## Measure the right website event

A form submission should be recorded at a genuine successful step, not merely when someone taps the button. Check that errors, repeat clicks and spam do not inflate the report.

Google recommends a `generate_lead` event for relevant lead-generation actions. Implementation still needs testing against your actual form. [Source: Google Analytics recommended events](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/collection/ga4/reference/events).

Our [contact-form guide](/blog/contact-form-questions-better-enquiries/) explains how the information collected should support the team's first response.

## Add the later sales outcome

Make recording a decision part of the normal quoting and sales process. Note whether the enquiry became a quote, a booking or an order, and why unsuitable requests were closed.

An illustrative campaign costing £300 and producing six qualified enquiries has a cost of £50 per qualified enquiry. If two become customers, that is £150 in campaign spend per acquired customer. Those calculations exclude other costs and are not a performance benchmark.

Look beyond revenue alone when comparing activity. Different jobs can carry different delivery costs, payment timing and staff demands. Keep the definitions consistent before deciding where to spend more.

## Account for incomplete information

Customers may discover you through several places, return later, use different devices or decline optional tracking. A customer who says “Google” may mean a search advert, a map result or an organic listing.

Treat attribution as a useful view of the journey rather than a perfect reconstruction. Combine available analytics with the team's records and customer answers. Keep unknown sources visible instead of forcing every enquiry into a guessed channel.

## Keep personal information out of Analytics

Do not send email addresses, phone numbers or free-text enquiry contents into analytics event parameters or page URLs. Google prohibits sending information it recognises as personally identifiable. [Source: Google Analytics privacy guidance](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/6366371?hl=en).

Review the tracking and consent setup alongside the business's privacy requirements. The sales system and the analytics platform do not need identical data.

## Make reporting lead to a decision

Review a manageable set of numbers: qualified enquiries, progression, customers, relevant value and spend. Ask what needs changing, who owns it and when you will check the result.

# Website maintenance: what should happen after launch?

Planned Monday: 2026-11-23 | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Website Maintenance: What Should Happen After Launch?

Meta description: Learn what website maintenance should cover after launch: access, backups, updates, form checks, renewals, support responsibilities and useful reporting.

Card excerpt: Understand the difference between hosting, maintenance and ongoing improvement, then agree who looks after the website after launch.

The website is live and the final invoice is paid. A month later, an enquiry form stops delivering, an old staff member still has access and nobody is certain which account renews the domain.

**The short answer:** after launch, your website needs clear ownership, reliable recovery arrangements, appropriate technical upkeep and regular checks of its customer journeys. Hosting provides somewhere for it to run. Maintenance and improvement are separate responsibilities that should be described in your agreement.

## Separate the services in the proposal

A single monthly payment can cover several different things. Ask the supplier to explain them in plain terms so you know what is included.

| Service | Typical purpose | Useful question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hosting or platform subscription | Keeps the site running on its chosen infrastructure | Who controls the account and renewals? |
| Maintenance | Handles agreed technical upkeep and checks | Which tasks, systems and response arrangements are included? |
| Content support | Makes agreed changes to information and assets | How much work is included and what is excluded? |
| Growth work | Tests and improves visibility or customer journeys | What outcomes and deliverables are being reviewed? |

The actual scope depends on the platform and contract. Do not assume each label includes everything in the table.

## Keep control of the important accounts

Know where the domain is registered, how hosting is paid for and who has administrator access. Include relevant analytics, form-delivery and booking accounts in the handover.

Use appropriate access controls and remove access when people no longer need it. Recovery details should belong to the business or be held through a clearly agreed arrangement. A forgotten personal email account can become a practical barrier when something needs changing.

## Ask how recovery works

Find out what is backed up, how often, where it is stored and how it can be restored. A site export, a database backup and a record of customer enquiries may be different things.

The NCSC includes backing up important information in its guidance for small organisations. Your website arrangements should support the business's wider recovery needs. [Source: NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/small-organisations-guide-to-cyber-security/backing-up-your-data).

Ask for an appropriate restoration check. A backup that exists but has never been tested leaves an important question unanswered.

## Match technical upkeep to the platform

A self-managed content system may require software and extension updates. A hosted platform may manage parts of the infrastructure while your team still owns content, integrations, account access and configuration.

Ask what is your responsibility and what the platform handles. Review changes in a suitable environment when needed, keep a record and check essential tasks afterwards. “The provider handles security” is not a complete description of every business responsibility.

## Check the routes customers rely on

Periodically submit a controlled form test, check receipt and confirm the customer acknowledgement. Open the booking calendar, follow important links and review key pages on a phone.

A useful check follows the customer task rather than only checking whether the homepage is online. An external widget or form service can fail while the rest of the site continues to load normally.

## Keep information current

Review services, prices where published, opening hours, contact details and staff information. Remove expired offers and old booking instructions. Give seasonal notices an end date.

Check images, documents and links that may have changed elsewhere. Keeping the information trustworthy is an ongoing task even when the underlying website code needs few changes.

## Define how support works

Agree how to report a problem, which issues are covered, who assesses urgency and what response times mean. A response acknowledgement and a completed fix are different commitments.

Clarify the difference between a defect, a small content edit and a new feature. If you want a new booking process, that may be a project rather than routine maintenance. Understanding the boundary makes the arrangement easier to manage.

## Review evidence of the work

Ask for a concise record of checks, changes and issues that need a decision. A maintenance report should explain what was done and what remains, rather than simply presenting a score.

# AI chatbot or live chat: which belongs on your small business website?

Planned Monday: 2026-11-30 | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: AI Chatbot vs Live Chat: A Small Business Guide

Meta description: Compare AI chatbots, rule-based chat and staffed live chat for a small business. Understand knowledge, handover, coverage, costs and practical testing.

Card excerpt: Choose website chat around the questions customers ask, the coverage your team can provide and a dependable route to a person.

A chat bubble promises an immediate answer. The customer opens it, asks about an unusual appointment and waits. There is no person available, and the automated reply sends them back to the page they already read.

**The short answer:** use automated chat for questions it can answer reliably, and staffed chat where the conversation needs judgement or a useful discussion. Choose around real customer needs, current information and available coverage. A clear handover is as important as the first answer.

## Understand the different approaches

A rule-based chatbot follows configured choices or rules. An AI chatbot generates responses using a model and the information or tools provided. Staffed live chat connects the visitor with a person, either in the business or through an agreed service.

A hybrid setup can combine them. Moneypenny's UK live-chat guide describes the distinction between automated responses and human conversations, including handover. [Further reading: Moneypenny](https://www.moneypenny.com/uk/resources/blog/the-complete-guide-to-live-chat-uk/).

The labels do not tell you whether the service is suitable. Ask what the system will actually do on your website and who takes responsibility when it reaches a limit.

## Start with the recurring questions

Review recent enquiries. Which questions can be answered from approved information? Which need someone to assess a particular situation? Which are signs that the website itself is unclear?

If people repeatedly ask where you work, improve the service-area information as well as any chat response. A chat tool should not become the only place customers can find an essential fact.

For a hypothetical coaching business, session locations and preparation instructions may be straightforward. Suitability for an individual with complex requirements may need a conversation with the coach.

## Compare the operating requirements

| Requirement | Automated chat | Staffed live chat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accurate routine information | Needs an approved, maintained source | Needs a team with the right information |
| Complex or sensitive requests | Needs a defined limit and handover | Needs a capable person and appropriate process |
| Availability | Depends on the service and system operation | Depends on genuine staffed coverage |
| Booking or account actions | Needs suitable, tested integrations | Needs authorised access and clear procedures |
| Ongoing upkeep | Knowledge, permissions and behaviour checks | Staffing, training and quality checks |

Both approaches need ownership. Neither becomes effective simply by adding a widget.

## Keep the knowledge current

List the information the tool may use: services, coverage, opening hours, booking rules and approved explanations of price. Decide who updates it when the business changes.

Avoid giving a system an unrestricted collection of outdated documents and assuming it will choose correctly. An explanation of a service from last year may conflict with the offer available today.

Specify what it should do when information is missing. A useful response can acknowledge the limit, collect relevant details and route the request to a person. Inventing an answer makes a fast conversation less valuable.

## Design the handover before the greeting

Decide which requests need a person, how that person is contacted and what information accompanies the handover. Tell the customer what happens if nobody is available.

Preserve useful context so the customer does not need to repeat everything. Provide another contact route when chat fails or is unsuitable. If the service is automated, make that clear rather than presenting it as a named staff member who does not exist.

## Test real tasks and awkward cases

Ask about an unavailable service, an out-of-area postcode, a changed opening time and a booking that cannot be accommodated. Try misspellings and ordinary customer language.

Check the result on a phone and with keyboard navigation. Make sure the widget does not cover an important button. Review what is stored, who can access conversations and the privacy information customers receive.

## Measure useful conversations

Track whether the visitor's question was resolved, whether a handover worked and whether the resulting enquiry was suitable. Include staff checking time, subscription charges and usage costs when assessing value.

A conversation count alone cannot tell you whether chat helped. Repeated questions may reveal a knowledge gap or a confusing service page. Use that information to improve the wider journey.

# How to create useful service-area pages without duplicating your website

Planned Monday: 2026-12-07 | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Service-Area Pages: How to Make Each One Useful

Meta description: Build useful service-area pages with accurate coverage, local project evidence and practical details. Learn when a separate location page adds real value.

Card excerpt: Decide when a location deserves its own page, what local information to include and how to avoid publishing the same page repeatedly.

Your business serves Brentwood, Billericay and several surrounding towns. Should the website contain a page for every place, or one page explaining the whole service area? The answer depends on what each page would help a customer understand.

**The short answer:** create a separate location page when it gives customers useful, accurate information about the service in that area. If the only difference is the town name, improve the existing coverage page first. A location page needs a purpose beyond repeating a search phrase.

## Distinguish a service area from an office

A business can travel to customers without having premises in every town it serves. Explain where you are based and where you work. Avoid presenting a coverage area as a staffed branch.

Google's business-profile guidelines require accurate representation of addresses and service areas. A website location page does not create eligibility for another business profile or an office that does not exist. [Source: Google Business Profile guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en).

A clear statement such as “Based in Brentwood, serving customers across the surrounding area” can be more useful than a set of invented office descriptions.

## Ask what changes for the customer

A separate page may be justified by a genuine difference in the service, access arrangements, availability or the type of work you undertake there. Relevant completed projects can also provide useful local context.

For a hypothetical garden-design business, one area may contain several completed courtyard projects with particular access considerations. That creates something specific to explain. Simply changing “garden design in Brentwood” to another town name does not.

## Choose the right page structure

| Situation | Sensible starting point |
| --- | --- |
| One service delivered consistently across a small area | A strong service page with clear coverage |
| Several services and a broad operating area | Service pages plus a useful coverage overview |
| A genuine local difference or substantial relevant project evidence | Consider a dedicated location page |
| Many near-identical pages with little useful detail | Review, consolidate or improve them |

This is an editorial decision, not a fixed quota of pages every business needs.

## Include information that helps someone buy

Explain which services are available in the area and any practical limits. Describe the first step, the information you need and how appointments or visits are arranged. Publish lead times only when you can keep them current.

Where you have permission, include real local work and explain the relevant challenge. A short, accurate example can give a customer more confidence than several paragraphs praising the town.

If travel charges or access requirements affect the quote, explain the process. Do not invent local pricing bands solely to make a page appear different.

## Use genuine local evidence

A project photograph should show the work described. A customer quote should be authentic and used appropriately. Avoid attaching the same review to several locations in a way that suggests several different customers.

If you have no relevant local project yet, describe your actual coverage and service process honestly. You can add more specific evidence after completing work. A useful page does not need fabricated proof.

## Avoid pages created only to funnel visitors elsewhere

Google describes doorway abuse as pages created to rank for similar queries and lead people to a less useful intermediate destination. Its examples include regional or city-targeted pages that funnel users to one page. [Source: Google Search Central spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies).

That does not mean all location pages are prohibited. It means the page should genuinely serve the visitor's task. Keep it useful in its own right and provide a clear, relevant next step.

## Connect the pages sensibly

Link from the coverage overview to the pages that exist, and connect local information with the relevant service. Use labels that explain the destination.

Avoid an enormous footer list that exists only to repeat location keywords. Review whether a customer can navigate the coverage naturally. Check the phone number and enquiry destination on every page.

## Review what the pages actually achieve

Look at the searches bringing people to each page, the information they use and the suitability of their enquiries. Keep availability and coverage current as the business changes.

# How to turn a finished project into a case study customers can trust

Planned Monday: 2026-12-14 | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: How to Write a Customer Case Study People Can Trust

Meta description: Create a trustworthy customer case study using the original brief, real project evidence, client approval and clearly explained outcomes or limitations.

Card excerpt: Turn a completed project into useful buying evidence by showing the brief, decisions, delivered work and outcomes you can substantiate.

You have finished a project you are proud of. The photographs look good, but the website still says only “another happy customer”. A potential buyer needs to know what the customer asked for, what you did and why the work is relevant to them.

**The short answer:** a useful case study explains the customer's situation, the work delivered and the outcome you can support with evidence. Get the right permissions, separate measured results from observations and give the reader enough context to judge whether your experience fits their needs.

## Choose a project that answers a buying question

Start with the question your next customer is likely to ask. Can you handle a small space? Can you work around occupied premises? Can you make a complicated service easier to book?

A project is useful when it demonstrates the relevant capability. The largest project is not always the best example. Choose a clear story connected with the service and customer group you want to attract.

## Gather the evidence before writing

Collect the original brief, scope, dates, relevant photographs or screenshots and a record of what was delivered. Check which details are confidential and what the client has agreed you may publish.

If you intend to discuss results, locate the underlying records and establish the period they cover. A comment that the new process feels easier is useful feedback, but it is not a measured reduction in processing time.

## Build the story around four questions

| Question | Useful material |
| --- | --- |
| What was the starting problem? | The customer's goal and constraints |
| What did you decide to do? | The approach and reasons behind it |
| What was delivered? | Specific work, with relevant visual evidence |
| What changed? | Supported outcomes, feedback and any limits |

This structure is familiar because it helps a buyer understand cause, action and outcome. Orbit Media's guide also discusses the challenge, solution and results format. [Further reading: Orbit Media](https://www.orbitmedia.com/blog/how-to-write-a-customer-success-story/).

## Show the decision, not only the finished image

For a hypothetical kitchen fitter, the interesting detail may be how storage was improved in a narrow room while preserving access. For a consultancy, it may be how a new enquiry route separated different types of request.

Describe the constraint and the choice it led to. Caption the images so someone can see why they matter. Avoid filling the page with technical details that do not affect the customer's decision.

## Be precise about numbers

If you measured a change, state what was counted, the dates, the baseline and any important differences between periods. Distinguish enquiries from customers and revenue from profit.

Suppose an illustrative business records ten completed forms before a change and 15 afterwards. That is five additional forms and a 50% increase, but it does not prove the website caused the increase or that five extra customers were acquired. Traffic, seasonality and other marketing may also have changed.

If you do not have reliable measurement, explain the delivered functionality and use attributed feedback. “The form now routes wholesale requests to the trade team” is a different claim from “the website doubled trade revenue”. Both require evidence appropriate to the statement.

## Use client feedback accurately

Ask the client what the experience was like and what changed for them. Preserve the meaning of their words and agree any edits before publication. Confirm how their name, role and business may be presented.

A short, specific observation is more useful than generic praise. If a review is also being requested on Google, follow a fair process rather than selecting only positive experiences. Our [review request guide](/blog/ask-for-google-reviews/) explains the distinction.

## Give the reader a relevant next step

Link the case study to the service it demonstrates and invite a conversation about a similar requirement. Do not suggest that every future customer will receive the same result.

Make the page easy to scan: a clear project summary, a few meaningful sections and useful images. Keep it current if the service, client relationship or underlying result changes.

## Turn delivery into an ongoing source of content

Build evidence collection into project close-out. Record the brief and decisions while they are still easy to recall, agree permissions and set a later review point for results that need time to develop.

Your team closes for a few days over Christmas, but the calendar still offers appointments. The website promises a reply within one working day. An automated message says someone will call shortly. Each system appears to work, but together they promise something the business cannot deliver.

**The short answer:** treat holiday availability as one connected update. Confirm what the business can provide, reflect it across every customer contact point and test the complete booking and enquiry journey. Make clear when a person will respond and when normal service resumes.

## Agree the operating plan first

Write down which dates you are open, which services remain available and who is covering enquiries. Separate the hours customers can visit from the hours someone monitors messages or delivers a service.

For a hypothetical maintenance business, planned visits may pause while an agreed urgent-response service continues. The customer needs that distinction, including any limits. “Open over Christmas” may be too vague to explain what is available.

Assign one owner to coordinate the updates and record when temporary information should be removed.

## Check every place a customer sees availability

| Contact point | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Website and service pages | Clear holiday dates and relevant service limits |
| Google Business Profile | Accurate special hours or other appropriate status |
| Booking calendar | Closures, reduced capacity and available appointment types |
| Form acknowledgements | A realistic date or timeframe for a human response |
| Phone and voicemail | Current hours and an appropriate alternative route |
| AI or chat tools | The same approved availability information and handover rules |

Include any other channel your customers actually use. One accurate notice cannot correct a conflicting promise elsewhere.

## Update the Google profile appropriately

Google provides special hours for short-term adjustments and closures of up to six consecutive days. Its guidance recommends confirming holiday hours even when they match normal hours. [Source: Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6303076?hl=en-GB).

For a longer closure or a different operating arrangement, check the current guidance for the appropriate profile setting rather than forcing every situation into special hours. Then review what a customer can actually see.

## Test the calendar's behaviour

Block dates when appointments cannot take place and review reduced staffing or different appointment types. Check the latest bookable slot before closure and the first one after reopening.

Try rescheduling an existing booking as well as making a new one. Confirmations and reminders should reflect the actual appointment, not an old default. If an external calendar manages availability, check that the website is using the updated information.

## Make acknowledgements useful

A receipt can confirm that an enquiry has arrived while explaining when it will be reviewed. Give a specific, accurate reopening date or response expectation where you can.

For illustration: “Thanks for your enquiry. Our office is closed until [reopening date]. We will review new requests when the team returns. Existing customers needing the agreed out-of-hours service should use [approved contact route].”

Replace every bracketed detail with real information before using the message. Do not describe an inbox as monitored or a service as urgent-response unless the arrangement exists.

## Update automated tools as well as the website

Review the information used by your chatbot, AI receptionist and follow-up workflows. A customer should not receive one answer from the page and a different answer from the assistant.

Decide which automations should pause. A sequence chasing a quote may need different timing if the business cannot answer the reply. Keep genuine service communications useful and avoid sending requests that create work nobody is covering.

## Make the notice easy to use

Put the essential information where someone making contact will see it. Keep the wording short and connect it with the affected action. A booking-page notice should explain the effect on bookings.

Check the mobile layout and keyboard access. A large pop-up that hides the enquiry form can create another obstacle. Give temporary notices a clear removal or review date.

## Run one complete customer test

Follow the journey from Google or a service page through the calendar or form to the confirmation. Check the destination inbox and the information the team receives.

# How to build a practical 90-day digital marketing plan for your small business

Planned Monday: 2026-12-28 | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Build a Practical 90-Day Digital Marketing Plan

Meta description: Build a practical 90-day digital marketing plan for your small business. Set one objective, prioritise useful work and measure enquiries and customer outcomes.

Card excerpt: Start the new year with one clear objective, a manageable set of priorities and a simple way to decide what is working.

January can produce a long list of marketing intentions: post more often, rebuild the website, run adverts, try AI and improve SEO. Without a clear objective, the list becomes a set of disconnected jobs competing with normal client work.

**The short answer:** choose one business objective, identify the main obstacle to it and plan a manageable set of actions over 90 days. Give each action an owner, a budget or time allowance and a way to assess progress. Review the evidence before adding more activity.

## Begin with the business result

Decide what you want the next quarter to achieve. More suitable enquiries for a particular service? More appointments from existing traffic? A more dependable pipeline for a team with spare capacity?

Be specific about the service and customer. A hypothetical consultancy might want more enquiries for a defined assessment package rather than “more engagement”. Agree how many requests the team could realistically handle.

Hallam's discussion of segmentation and targeting is a useful reminder that choosing the audience is part of strategy. A small business needs a practical focus as much as a larger organisation does. [Further reading: Hallam](https://hallam.agency/blog/revisiting-segmentation-and-targeting/).

## Establish a starting point

Review recent qualified enquiries, their sources, the services requested and what happened next. Note gaps in the information rather than inventing a precise baseline.

Look for an obvious constraint. You may have enough visitors but unclear service information, or enough enquiries but slow follow-up. These situations call for different work. Use our [lead-tracking guide](/blog/track-marketing-paying-customers/) to connect activity with customer outcomes.

## Choose a small number of priorities

Pick the actions most likely to address the constraint. Explain why each matters before committing resources. A new channel is not automatically the best next step.

For example, a business losing suitable enquiries at the booking stage could first simplify that journey, confirm availability and improve the follow-up process. Paying for more traffic before checking those steps may increase the workload without resolving the problem.

## Organise the quarter into three stages

| Stage | Main question | Example work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Days 1–30: understand and repair | Can customers take the intended next step? | Check tracking, clarify the offer and fix a broken enquiry route |
| Days 31–60: deliver a focused test | Does the chosen activity reach suitable buyers? | Publish relevant evidence or test a tightly scoped campaign |
| Days 61–90: review and improve | What should we continue, change or stop? | Compare outcomes, refine the journey and plan the next quarter |

These stages are a planning structure, not a promise that search rankings or revenue will change within a fixed period.

## Give each action a workable brief

Write down the audience, purpose, deliverable, owner and due date. Include dependencies such as approved photographs, access to the booking system or a client's permission to publish a case study.

For content, define the buying question being answered and the next step it supports. Our [case-study guide](/blog/customer-case-study-guide/) explains how completed work can become useful evidence instead of another generic article.

Keep the plan realistic alongside delivery commitments. A smaller set of completed actions is easier to evaluate than a large list of half-finished ideas.

## Set limits on spending and staff time

Agree what the business can commit, including supplier costs, advertising spend, software and internal hours. Keep a clear distinction between the cost of setup and the cost of ongoing operation.

For an illustrative test, define a maximum spend and a review point before it begins. Decide what evidence would justify continuing. Avoid treating a forecast as money already earned or using a universal percentage of turnover without checking the business's circumstances.

## Choose measures that support decisions

Use a small set of relevant outcomes: qualified enquiries, bookings attended, quotes accepted and customers acquired. Add a few supporting indicators that help explain the result, such as relevant visits or form completion.

Record what changed and when. If a new page, campaign and sales process all launch together, be careful about attributing the whole outcome to one element. Keep unknowns visible.

## Build in a short review routine

Check delivery progress regularly and review meaningful outcomes at agreed points. Ask what has been learned, what needs a decision and who will act. Avoid changing direction after every small fluctuation.

At the end of the quarter, keep the work that is useful, stop activity that no longer has a case and set the next priorities from the evidence. Include ongoing [website care](/blog/website-maintenance-after-launch/) so improvements remain dependable.
