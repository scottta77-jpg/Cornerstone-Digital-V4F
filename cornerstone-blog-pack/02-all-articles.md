# Why your website isn't generating enquiries: 9 checks before a redesign

Web design | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Website Not Generating Enquiries? 9 Checks to Make

Meta description: Find out why your website gets visitors but few enquiries. Use nine practical checks to spot problems with messaging, trust, forms and follow-up.

Card excerpt: Getting website visitors but few enquiries? Check your message, proof, contact journey and follow-up before paying for a redesign.

Your website looks presentable. People visit it. Yet the enquiries are inconsistent, low quality or missing altogether. Before replacing the whole site, find out where the journey breaks.

**The short answer:** a website can lose enquiries because it attracts the wrong visitors, leaves important questions unanswered, makes contact difficult or fails to pass leads to someone who will respond. Check each stage separately. A new design only helps if it fixes the underlying problem.

## 1. Are the right people arriving?

Open your analytics and look at the pages people enter through. Compare these with the services you actually want to sell. Someone reading a general advice article may have a different intention from someone searching for a quote.

Check geography as well. A Brentwood service business can receive visitors from across the country while needing customers within a manageable travel area. More traffic is useful only when it includes people you can serve.

## 2. Does the first screen explain the offer?

Show your homepage to someone unfamiliar with the business. Ask what you do, who it is for and where you work. If they need an explanation, the opening message needs work.

For a hypothetical heating company, “Boiler repairs and servicing in Brentwood” gives a customer more information than “Excellence through innovation”. Follow it with a relevant reason to choose you and a clear action.

## 3. Do your service pages answer buying questions?

A list of services leaves customers to fill in the gaps. Explain what is included, what happens next, where you operate and any limits that affect suitability. Add a genuine project example where you have one.

If price varies, explain the main cost drivers and how you prepare a quote. A useful explanation of pricing can remove uncertainty without promising a fixed price for every job.

## 4. Can people check that you are credible?

Use real work, attributable reviews and relevant qualifications. Put the evidence near the service or decision it supports. An example of a completed kitchen installation belongs beside kitchen installation information.

Avoid invented numbers and anonymous praise that could describe any company. A project description with a clear brief, photographs and an explanation of the work is more useful than an unsupported claim to be the area's leading provider.

## 5. Is there an obvious next step?

Choose the action that fits the sale. An emergency service may need a prominent phone number. A consultancy may need a discovery call. A quote-based business may need a short enquiry form.

Explain what follows the click. “Request a quote” becomes easier to act on when the page also explains what information you need and when a person will respond. Only publish response times your team can meet.

## 6. Does the mobile journey work?

Use your own phone to find a service, read its details and make contact. Watch for sticky elements covering buttons, menus that are hard to close and text that needs excessive zooming.

Test the journey on an ordinary connection. Performance scores can help diagnose issues, but the practical question is whether someone can complete the task comfortably. Our [Core Web Vitals guide](/blog/core-web-vitals-small-business/) explains what to measure.

## 7. Does the form actually deliver?

Submit a real test enquiry. Check the success message, destination inbox, spam folder and customer acknowledgement. Confirm that someone knows who owns the reply.

Ask only for information needed at this stage. If a customer must complete a lengthy questionnaire before asking a basic question, consider whether those details can wait until the conversation begins.

## 8. What happens after an enquiry arrives?

An instant acknowledgement confirms receipt. It does not resolve the customer's request. Give the enquiry an owner and provide a realistic next step.

If missed calls are the main gap, compare [AI receptionists and missed-call text-back](/blog/ai-receptionist-vs-missed-call-text-back/) before choosing a tool. A simple callback process may be enough for a low-volume business.

## 9. Are you counting meaningful outcomes?

Record successful form submissions, booked consultations and qualified enquiries. A phone-link click shows interest, but does not prove a call connected or became a customer. Where possible, connect marketing activity with the later sales outcome.

For illustration, 12 qualified enquiries from 600 visits is a 2% qualified-enquiry rate. That arithmetic describes an example, not a target or industry benchmark. Compare your own periods using consistent definitions.

## Fix the weakest stage first

Write down one problem at each stage: finding you, understanding you, trusting you, contacting you and getting a response. Prioritise the clearest obstacle and measure what changes. That gives a redesign a practical brief if one is needed.

# How much does a small business website cost? A UK buying guide

Strategy | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Small Business Website Cost: A UK Buying Guide

Meta description: Compare small business website costs in the UK. Understand scope, monthly plans, running costs, ownership and the questions to ask before buying.

Card excerpt: Understand what changes a website quote, how monthly plans compare and which ongoing costs to include before choosing a supplier.

Two businesses can ask for a five-page website and receive very different quotes. One needs a straightforward introduction and contact form. The other needs copywriting, booking rules, photography and a connection to its customer database.

**The short answer:** website cost depends on the work included, the way you pay and the ongoing services you need. Compare the complete scope and total cost over a realistic period. A page count and headline price are not enough to judge value.

## What do published prices tell you?

As one dated example, Waywest Design's Leeds guide lists £1,500 to £3,000 for a starter brochure site and £3,000 to £6,000 for a growth CMS site, based on its 2025 projects. These are that supplier's published bands, not a UK market average or a Cornerstone Digital quote. [Source: Waywest Design](https://waywestdesign.co.uk/website-cost-leeds-2025/), checked 21 October 2026.

A lower-priced offer can cover a narrower scope, a standardised process or a different payment model. A higher quote may include more research, content and support. Ask what you receive before comparing the numbers.

## What usually changes the scope?

**Content.** Who writes the service pages, chooses images and checks the facts? Supplying finished content is different from asking an agency to research, write and organise it.

**Customer journeys.** A single contact form is different from online ordering, deposits, availability-based bookings or multiple enquiry types with different destinations.

**Design and branding.** Adapting a suitable template takes a different amount of work from creating a new visual identity, bespoke layouts and original assets.

**Existing website changes.** Moving content, preserving useful URLs and mapping redirects add work that a new business may not need.

**Connections and support.** Email delivery, analytics, CRM connections, accessibility work, staff training and post-launch assistance should be described explicitly.

## Upfront payment or a monthly plan?

An upfront build separates the initial project cost from running costs. A monthly arrangement can spread payment and may include support. Both can work if the terms are clear.

For a hypothetical comparison, Supplier A charges £1,200 to build and £25 a month to run the site. Supplier B charges £300 to start and £75 a month. Over 24 months, the totals are £1,800 and £2,100 respectively, assuming the same tax treatment and no additional charges. The services may still differ.

Those figures are an illustration, not current Cornerstone pricing. Ask about the minimum term, cancellation, annual price changes and what happens to the website when payments stop.

## Which ongoing costs should you budget for?

Ask for a clear list covering your domain renewal, hosting or platform subscription, paid tools, maintenance, backups and support. Payment processing, premium booking software or usage-based AI services can have separate charges.

Check what “updates included” means. Technical maintenance and changing your opening hours are different tasks from adding a new service section or rewriting the website.

If a quote mentions SEO, ask which deliverables are included. Setting page titles and submitting a sitemap is a different scope from ongoing content, local visibility work and reporting.

## What should you own or control?

Find out who controls the domain, hosting account, analytics and relevant software subscriptions. Ask what access you receive and whether content, customer data and code can be exported or transferred.

Ownership of commissioned work does not automatically remove the terms of a third-party platform or asset licence. The practical question is whether you can keep operating the site and move suppliers on understood terms.

## Send every supplier the same brief

List the services you sell, the customers and locations you serve, the pages required and the action visitors should take. Specify who supplies the copy and images. Include any booking, payment or enquiry-routing requirements.

Then ask each supplier for the build cost, recurring costs, exclusions, delivery dependencies and handover terms. A like-for-like brief makes differences in value easier to see.

A business that needs clear service pages and reliable contact forms may not need a complex platform. A business taking orders or managing bookings needs a more detailed specification. Choose the scope around the customer task and the capacity of your team.

# AI receptionist vs missed-call text-back: which does your business need?

AI & automation | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: AI Receptionist vs Missed-Call Text-Back: UK Guide

Meta description: Should you use an AI receptionist or missed-call text-back? Compare the jobs they handle, limitations, costs and checks for a UK service business.

Card excerpt: Compare live AI call answering with automatic text replies, including when each helps, what to test and how to measure the value.

A customer phones while you are with another client. You cannot answer. The question is what should happen next: should software handle the call, or should a text message open a route to a person?

**The short answer:** an AI receptionist answers and holds a voice conversation. Missed-call text-back sends a message after an unanswered call. Choose voice handling when callers need routine answers or booking help; consider text-back when the priority is collecting details and arranging a callback.

## What can an AI receptionist do?

Depending on its configuration and integrations, it can answer approved questions, collect contact details, check service areas, create an enquiry and offer available appointments. The system needs current business information and clear limits.

Booking is not automatic simply because a tool uses AI. It requires a working calendar connection, accurate availability and rules for appointment length, location and cancellation. Confirm what happens when the connection fails.

Moneypenny's UK guide also describes human handover as part of call handling. That is a useful design principle: some calls need judgement or reassurance beyond a routine script. [Source: Moneypenny](https://www.moneypenny.com/uk/resources/blog/ai-receptionist-complete-guide).

## What does missed-call text-back do?

A connected phone system detects an unanswered call and sends a short message to a suitable number. The customer can reply with the information you need, and your team can continue the conversation.

A hypothetical message for a local maintenance business could read: “Thanks for calling Oakfield Maintenance. We couldn't answer just now. Reply with your postcode and what you need help with, and our team will get back to you during opening hours.”

Text-back does not answer the original call, guarantee a reply or turn every number into an SMS contact. Test withheld numbers, landlines, repeated calls and messages that fail to deliver.

## Compare the job you need done

| Need | AI receptionist | Missed-call text-back |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speak to a caller immediately | Can handle configured voice conversations | Follows up after the call |
| Collect basic enquiry details | During the conversation | Through the customer's reply |
| Book appointments | Requires a tested calendar integration | Can share a booking link or request a callback |
| Manage complex or sensitive requests | Needs a reliable human handover | Needs a person to review and respond |
| Work with callers who cannot use SMS | Voice may be suitable | Offer another contact route |

## Start with your actual missed calls

Review a normal fortnight. How many calls were missed? How many were new enquiries, existing customers or spam? How quickly did someone return them? Which questions came up repeatedly?

If the missed volume is small, a better callback routine may solve the problem. If routine calls regularly interrupt paid work, a call-handling system may deserve a trial. After-hours demand is another reason to review coverage, provided callers understand what can actually be resolved.

## What should you test before going live?

Try a postcode outside your area, an unclear address, a request for an unavailable service and an attempt to book an occupied slot. Ask the same question in different ways. Check that the system does not invent a price or promise a service you do not offer.

Test human handover and failure alerts. Urgent situations need an approved escalation process; do not rely on an AI system to make an unsupported safety decision. Make clear to callers when they are interacting with an automated assistant.

## Keep customer data and messages under control

Decide what information is collected, who can access transcripts, how long records are kept and what suppliers do with them. Provide appropriate privacy information and review the setup before recording or transcribing calls.

A reply to an enquiry and a promotional campaign are different communications. The ICO explains that adding promotional material to a service message can make it direct marketing. Do not treat an inbound call as blanket permission for later marketing. [Source: ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/direct-marketing-guidance/identify-direct-marketing/).

## Measure the trial against the full cost

Compare qualified enquiries captured, appointments attended, complaints and staff time with the baseline. Include setup, subscription, minutes, messages and maintenance. A fast response has little value if the information is wrong or the handover fails.

# Local SEO and AI search: how small businesses can be easier to find

Search & growth | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Local SEO and AI Search: A Small Business Guide

Meta description: Improve how customers find and understand your business through local SEO and AI search. Learn which website, business profile and content fixes matter.

Card excerpt: A practical guide to clear service pages, accurate business details and useful answers that help customers understand what you offer.

A customer might search Google for a nearby service, look at a map, read reviews or ask an AI assistant to compare options. Your business needs clear, reliable information wherever that research happens.

**The short answer:** begin with accurate business details, useful service pages, accessible content and evidence of real work. These foundations help customers and search systems understand what you offer. SEO, AEO and GEO describe overlapping visibility work; none guarantees a ranking, citation or recommendation.

## What do SEO, AEO and GEO mean?

SEO means search engine optimisation. AEO, or answer engine optimisation, usually describes making answers easy to find and understand. GEO, or generative engine optimisation, describes work intended to improve visibility in AI-generated answers.

Google's guidance says its established SEO practices remain relevant to AI search and that no special AI schema is required. Other platforms have their own systems, so avoid treating one tactic as a universal rule. [Source: Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features).

## Make each main service understandable

Give a customer enough information to decide whether to contact you. Explain the service, who it suits, what is included, where you deliver it and how the process works. Describe any important limits.

For a hypothetical property maintenance company, an emergency repair service and a planned maintenance service need different explanations. One may involve urgent availability; the other may require a site assessment and recurring schedule.

Where separate pages genuinely help those decisions, create them. Avoid making multiple near-identical pages just to repeat variations of a search phrase.

## Be precise about your service area

List the locations you actually cover. Use your real business name and accurate contact information consistently. An Essex-based company can explain that it serves Brentwood and surrounding areas without pretending to have an office in every town.

For eligible businesses, keep your Google Business Profile accurate. Google describes local results in terms of relevance, distance and prominence. A blog cannot remove the role of location in a local search. [Source: Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en).

## Answer the questions customers ask before buying

Useful questions include “What affects the price?”, “How long does the process take?”, “What do I need to provide?” and “Is this suitable for my situation?” Answer them directly, then explain the details that matter.

A concise opening answer helps a busy reader. There is no reason to force every answer into an identical word count or repeat “In summary” at the start of each section. Use the length the question needs.

If a customer needs a comparison, provide one. Explain when an option is unsuitable as well as when it works. That makes the article useful beyond repeating the name of your service.

## Add evidence that belongs to your business

Publish real project photographs, explain the work completed and use customer feedback with appropriate permission. A case study should separate the brief, the delivered work and the measured outcome.

If you have not measured an increase in enquiries, do not claim one. You can still explain how a new booking journey works, which decisions shaped it and what you will measure next.

Relevant third-party coverage and accurate profiles can also help people investigate a business. Seek genuine relationships and coverage connected with your work rather than mass-producing artificial endorsements.

## Check that the content is discoverable

Link important articles from the blog and relevant service pages. Make sure their content is available on a working page and that visitors can reach it through ordinary links.

Use descriptive page titles and headings. Add structured data that accurately reflects the visible content where appropriate, but do not treat markup as a substitute for a useful page. A technically valid page is not automatically selected for an AI answer.

## Measure visibility and the enquiries it produces

Use Search Console and analytics to review the queries, pages and referrals associated with your traffic. Record qualified enquiries and sales alongside those visits.

You can also maintain a small, repeatable set of customer questions to observe AI mentions. Record the platform, date and exact wording. Results can vary, so treat this as an observation sample rather than a fixed market ranking.

## Build a useful resource, then maintain it

Start with the questions most closely connected to buying your service. Link each article to the relevant next step and update it when the underlying information changes. The aim is to help a customer make a sound decision and contact the right business.

# 5 small business automations to start with, and how to measure them

Business automation | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: 5 Small Business Automations Worth Starting With

Meta description: Explore five practical small business automations. Learn what to connect, where human checks belong and how to calculate the time you actually save.

Card excerpt: Start with five everyday workflows: enquiry routing, appointment reminders, quote follow-ups, onboarding and customer feedback.

If the same enquiry gets copied from an inbox into a spreadsheet, then into a calendar, the problem may be the handover between tools. That is a useful place to investigate automation.

**The short answer:** begin with a frequent, predictable task that has clear inputs and an obvious owner. Connect one workflow, test its exceptions and measure the result. Time saved depends on your actual workload; a promise of 15 hours a week is meaningless without a baseline.

## 1. Enquiry capture and routing

When someone submits a form, create a record in the place your team manages enquiries. Include the service requested, contact details and relevant location. Send a receipt to the customer and assign the enquiry to a person.

Decide what happens if a field is missing or a connection fails. Keep an alert or fallback inbox so a technical error does not silently hide a new lead. Agree how duplicate submissions are handled.

**Measure:** enquiries assigned correctly, duplicate records and time spent transferring details manually. A receipt email is a useful step, but the enquiry still needs a real response.

## 2. Appointment confirmations and reminders

Connect confirmed bookings to a reminder process. Include the appointment time, location or joining instructions, and a clear way to reschedule. Choose timings around the way your customers prepare for the appointment.

A site visit may need access instructions. A consultation may need a short preparation note. A cancellation should stop later reminders, and a changed booking should update the message.

**Measure:** reminders delivered, attendance, rescheduling and customer confusion. Compare similar periods rather than assuming every change in attendance came from the automation.

## 3. Quote follow-ups

Give outstanding quotes an owner and a status. A follow-up can ask whether the customer received the quote or needs clarification. It should stop when they reply, accept, decline or ask you not to contact them.

Avoid treating email-open tracking as proof that a person read the proposal. Tracking can be incomplete or affected by privacy features. A reply or decision is a more meaningful signal.

**Measure:** quotes with a recorded outcome, staff time spent checking them and complaints about unnecessary messages. Apply the relevant messaging rules before adding promotional follow-ups.

## 4. Client onboarding

A confirmed acceptance or verified payment can trigger an agreed set of tasks: create a project record, assign an owner and send the next-step information. Request only the documents needed for that stage.

Check your trigger carefully. Starting delivery after an abandoned checkout or an unconfirmed payment can create work that should never have begun. Repeated notifications should not create duplicate projects.

**Measure:** time from confirmation to a complete project record, missing information and rework. Keep a person available for unusual requirements or conflicting instructions.

## 5. Feedback and review requests

After a completed service, send an appropriate request for feedback using consistent eligibility rules. Give customers a way to raise a problem and an equal opportunity to leave an honest public review.

Do not create a positive-only route to Google. Our [review request guide](/blog/ask-for-google-reviews/) explains how to avoid review gating. Keep review requests separate from discounts or marketing campaigns unless you have assessed the rules that apply.

**Measure:** requests delivered, useful feedback, reviews received and issues resolved. Avoid making a five-star rating the condition for a successful workflow.

## Work out whether an automation is worthwhile

Use your own numbers. Suppose a task occurs 30 times each week and takes four minutes. That is 120 minutes of manual work. If exceptions and checking still take 30 minutes after automation, the illustrative net saving is 90 minutes a week.

Now include the subscription, setup and ongoing maintenance. Consider the value of fewer errors or a more reliable response, but do not count hypothetical sales as money already earned. Track what actually changes.

## Give every workflow an owner

Write down the trigger, action, stop conditions and failure response. Test missing information, duplicate events, cancellations and unavailable services. Check who receives an alert and how they recover the task.

Use the simplest dependable approach. A clear rule may be suitable for moving data; AI may help interpret a free-text request, but needs checks where a wrong decision would matter. Review permissions and customer information before connecting systems.

# Core Web Vitals for small businesses: what to fix first

Website performance | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Core Web Vitals for Small Businesses: What to Fix

Meta description: Understand LCP, INP and CLS in plain English. Learn how to check Core Web Vitals and prioritise website fixes without chasing a perfect speed score.

Card excerpt: Understand loading, responsiveness and layout stability, then prioritise the fixes that affect real customer journeys.

A slow website is frustrating when a customer is trying to check a service, open a menu or submit an enquiry. Core Web Vitals help you examine parts of that experience, but they are more useful as a diagnosis than as a trophy score.

**The short answer:** check how quickly the main content appears, how promptly the page responds and whether its layout moves unexpectedly. Use real visitor data where available, investigate problems with controlled tests and fix the important customer journeys first.

## What are LCP, INP and CLS?

Google's web.dev documentation describes three Core Web Vitals. Their “good” thresholds are assessed at the 75th percentile of visits, considered separately for mobile and desktop:

| Metric | What it measures | Good threshold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Largest Contentful Paint, or LCP | When the largest visible content element appears | 2.5 seconds or less |
| Interaction to Next Paint, or INP | How quickly the page visually responds to interactions | 200 milliseconds or less |
| Cumulative Layout Shift, or CLS | Unexpected movement of page elements | 0.1 or less |

These are experience thresholds, not a requirement that every part of a page finish loading in under a second. [Source: web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/vitals).

## Begin with the pages customers actually use

Check the homepage, important service pages, popular articles and the booking or contact journey. A fast homepage does not tell you whether a booking widget becomes difficult to use on another page.

Use a phone as well as a desktop. Open menus, accept or reject optional cookies where relevant, navigate the page and complete the form. Record the problem you see before deciding how to fix it.

## Understand field data and lab tests

Field data reflects real visits across different devices and connections. A lab test runs in a controlled setup and helps reproduce a problem. They answer different questions, so the results may differ.

PageSpeed Insights can show available field data alongside lab diagnostics. Some smaller sites will not have enough public field data for a particular page. An absent result does not mean that the page has failed.

Use the diagnostics to identify likely causes, then check whether the change improves the experience. Avoid repeatedly tuning a page around one isolated test run.

## If the main content appears late

Investigate the element reported as LCP. Is it a large photograph, a banner or a block of text waiting for a font? Check the image size, delivery and loading behaviour, alongside server and rendering delays.

A large decorative video can add work without helping the customer make a decision. Consider whether a carefully chosen still image would serve the page better. Essential first-screen content should not be delayed by an unnecessary loading effect.

## If buttons and menus feel unresponsive

Look at the work the browser is doing around an interaction. Heavy scripts, complex animations and third-party widgets can contribute to delays. Remove tools that no longer serve a clear purpose.

Test essential integrations individually. A booking calendar or chat tool may be useful, but it still needs to work comfortably on an ordinary phone. Keep the customer action available if an optional widget fails.

## If the layout jumps while someone is using it

Reserve space for images, embeds and other content before it arrives. Watch banners, notices and font changes that unexpectedly move a button or paragraph.

A customer should not tap “Submit” and hit a different element because the layout changed. Reproduce the issue during the actual task, then confirm the fix on different screen sizes.

## Will passing guarantee better rankings?

No. Google says Core Web Vitals are used by its ranking systems, while good scores do not guarantee top rankings. Relevant, useful content and the wider search context still matter. [Source: Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals).

The commercial benefit also needs measurement. If a form becomes easier to use, track successful submissions and qualified enquiries over a suitable period. Do not assume a universal percentage increase for every second removed.

## A sensible order for improvements

Fix broken customer actions first. Then address clearly poor performance on valuable pages, remove unnecessary weight and repeat the same tests. Monitor field results as new visits accumulate.

Keep a record of what changed and when. That helps separate a real improvement from changes in traffic, campaigns or test conditions. Combine performance work with our [website enquiry checks](/blog/website-not-generating-enquiries/) so the page is clear as well as responsive.

# How to ask for Google reviews without review gating

Customer growth | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: How to Ask for Google Reviews Without Review Gating

Meta description: Learn how to ask customers for Google reviews, write a neutral request and automate follow-ups without filtering out unhappy customers or offering rewards.

Card excerpt: Create a fair review-request process with a useful message, sensible timing and an equal opportunity for customers to share honest feedback.

You finish the job, send the invoice and move on to the next customer. Asking for a review is easy to forget. A repeatable process can help, provided it invites honest feedback rather than selecting the people most likely to praise you.

**The short answer:** ask customers consistently after a genuine experience, make the review link easy to use and let them choose what to say. Do not offer a reward or give only happy customers a route to Google. Handle service problems alongside the review process.

## What is review gating?

Review gating commonly means checking a customer's sentiment before deciding whether to invite a public review. For example, a survey sends people scoring four or five stars to Google while directing lower scores only to a private form.

Google's contribution policy prohibits selectively soliciting positive reviews and discouraging negative ones. It also prohibits incentives in exchange for reviews. A workflow should therefore make the public review opportunity available on consistent terms. [Source: Google Maps contribution policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en).

## Who should receive the request?

Define a practical, neutral rule, such as customers whose booked service has been completed and whose contact details can appropriately be used. Apply it regardless of whether you expect the feedback to be positive.

Check for duplicate records, requests already sent and people who should no longer be contacted. Staff, suppliers and people without a genuine customer experience should not be recruited to manufacture praise.

## When should you ask?

Choose a point when the customer can assess the service. That might be shortly after a completed appointment or after a product has arrived and been used. There is no universal 24-hour rule for every business.

A request should make sense in the context of the purchase. Repeated reminders can become intrusive, so keep the process restrained and stop it when appropriate. Any messaging setup should account for the relevant privacy and electronic communications requirements.

## What should the message say?

Use clear, neutral language. For a hypothetical business, a request could read:

> Hi Sam, thank you for choosing Oakfield Maintenance. If you'd like to share your experience, you can leave an honest Google review here: [review link]. We appreciate your time and feedback. If you need help with your service, reply to this message and our team will assist.

Replace the example name and link with real details. Avoid asking for a specific star rating, supplying the customer's words or suggesting that support depends on leaving feedback.

## Can you offer a discount or prize?

Do not offer an incentive for a Google review, including a discount, free item or entry into a prize draw. Keep any referral or loyalty offer separate from whether someone writes, changes or removes a review.

A thank-you message after feedback does not need a reward attached. The process should work equally for praise, mixed experiences and criticism.

## What should happen when a customer has a problem?

Give them a straightforward way to contact the team and assign the issue to a person who can help. Investigate the concern without making removal of a review a condition of support.

If replying publicly, avoid disclosing private order details or personal information. Acknowledge the issue appropriately and move the detailed conversation to a suitable private channel. The service response matters independently of the rating.

## How can you automate this fairly?

Connect a genuine completion event to the request process. Use the same eligibility rule for everyone, prevent duplicate sends and keep a record of when a request was sent. If you collect private feedback, do not use its score to control access to the Google link.

Test cancelled bookings, duplicate invoices and customers who contact you for support. Decide who monitors replies. Automation should make the request reliable while keeping the business accountable for the conversation.

## What should you measure?

Track completed services, requests delivered, reviews received and issues raised. This helps you spot a broken link or a request sent at the wrong point in the journey. It also provides a clearer picture than counting five-star reviews alone.

Google says reviews can contribute to local prominence, alongside other factors. It does not provide a rule that a certain review frequency guarantees a map position or an AI recommendation. [Source: Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en).

# A practical website accessibility checklist for small businesses

Web design | 4 min read | Cornerstone Digital

SEO title: Website Accessibility Checklist for Small Businesses

Meta description: Use this practical website accessibility checklist to review navigation, readability, forms and booking journeys, then identify where expert testing is needed.

Card excerpt: Check whether customers can read, navigate and use your website, including menus, forms, booking tools and mobile layouts.

A website can look polished and still prevent someone from using it. A menu may be difficult to operate without a mouse. A form may rely on disappearing placeholder text. A booking tool may become unusable when the page is enlarged.

**The short answer:** check whether people can read the information, navigate the site and complete the main task using different ways of interacting. Start with real customer journeys. A short checklist identifies obvious barriers; it does not certify accessibility or replace a fuller assessment.

## Begin with one complete customer task

Choose a task such as finding a service and sending an enquiry. Include the menu, service page, form, confirmation and any external booking tool. A barrier at the final step can undo the effort put into the rest of the journey.

Write down what you tried, what happened and the device or browser used. A specific problem is easier to fix than a general note that a page feels difficult.

## Can you navigate without a mouse?

Put the mouse aside and try the keyboard. Can you reach links, open the menu, move through the form and close a dialog? Can you see which element has focus as you move?

Pay particular attention to pop-ups and embedded tools. If focus disappears or becomes trapped, record the step where it happened. [Reference: W3C WAI keyboard guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/keyboard.html).

## Does the content stay usable when enlarged?

Enlarge the page and check the important tasks again. Watch for text hidden behind fixed headers, buttons that disappear and form fields that become difficult to reach.

Try a narrow screen as well. Customers should be able to follow the reading order and understand the page without guessing which block comes next. Useful information should remain available when decorative layouts adapt.

## Are labels and instructions clear?

A form field needs an identifiable purpose. Explain what information is required and how to correct a mistake. Preserve entered information where practical so an error does not force someone to start again.

Do not rely only on a field's colour to explain its status. A message such as “Enter a valid email address” is more helpful than a red border alone. [Reference: W3C WAI form instructions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/instructions/).

## Can customers distinguish the text and controls?

Check small text, muted captions and text placed over photographs. A colour combination that looks attractive on a large monitor may be difficult to read elsewhere. Include button states, error messages and links in the review.

Use a contrast checker where appropriate rather than judging entirely by eye. Accessibility does not require abandoning a premium visual style; it requires making the information usable. [Reference: W3C WAI contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html).

## Do images and media communicate their purpose?

Describe informative images in a way that helps someone understand the relevant content. Decorative images should not create unnecessary repetition for assistive technology. Keep important instructions available as text rather than only inside an image.

If a video explains a service, consider the alternatives needed to access its information. Avoid forcing someone to listen to audio or watch animation to find a basic contact detail. [Reference: W3C WAI image guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/).

## Does the page structure make sense?

Use headings to organise the content and descriptive labels for links. A person scanning the page should be able to understand its main sections and what each action does.

Review repeated links such as “Learn more” in context. “View boiler servicing details” tells a reader more about the destination. Keep wording clear without filling every label with search phrases.

## What should you do with the findings?

Prioritise barriers that prevent people from completing an important task. Fix the problem and repeat the same journey. Include accessibility in later changes, especially new forms, content and third-party tools.

W3C's Easy Checks is an introductory review, not a complete evaluation. Use qualified testing, including relevant assistive technologies and user feedback, when a fuller assessment is needed. [Source: W3C WAI Easy Checks](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/preliminary/).

# Website builder or web designer? How to choose for your small business

A website builder gives you tools to create a site. A web designer provides a service that may include planning, content, design and implementation. These choices overlap: a professional can use a website builder, and a custom-coded site still needs a clear brief.

**The short answer:** choose an approach around the work required and who will do it. A straightforward site can suit a capable owner using a builder. A more involved customer journey may justify professional help. The platform name alone does not establish quality, value or suitability.

## Start with the customer task

Write down what visitors need to do. Read about your service and send an enquiry? Compare packages and book a consultation? Place a complex order? Those tasks create different requirements.

Then identify what your team can provide. Copywriting, image selection, page structure, form testing and keeping information current all need an owner. A tool can make parts easier without removing those jobs.

## When doing it yourself can make sense

A builder can be a practical option when the site is relatively simple, you have time to learn the tool and you are comfortable managing the content. You can make routine changes directly and work at your own pace.

The trade-off is your time and responsibility for decisions. Before starting, test the difficult part of your requirements, such as a booking rule or enquiry destination. Avoid choosing solely because a sample homepage looks attractive.

## When a web designer adds value

Professional help is useful when you need someone to turn an unclear brief into a coherent site, write or organise the content, establish a consistent design and test the customer journey.

Ask which of those jobs are included. Some designers focus on visual work; others provide strategy, implementation and support. The label is less useful than the deliverables, examples and responsibilities in the proposal.

## When more custom development is justified

A tailored implementation may be appropriate when your process requires unusual interactions, specific system connections or behaviour that an available platform cannot support sensibly.

That needs a clear specification, maintenance plan and handover. More custom work creates more choices to manage. It is worth doing when it solves an identified requirement and the business can support it after launch.

## Compare the approaches consistently

| Question | Build it yourself | Hire a designer or agency |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Who makes later changes? | You, within the tool's capabilities | You, the supplier or both, by agreement |

A professional service does not automatically mean a bespoke codebase. A builder does not automatically mean poor performance. Assess the finished work and how it serves the customer.

## Include the cost of your own time

Compare setup, ongoing charges and the hours you will spend planning, building and maintaining the site. Include help you may need when something stops working.

## Ask about control before you commit

Find out who controls the domain and accounts, what you can edit and which parts can be exported. Check cancellation and transfer terms, third-party subscriptions and what happens if the original supplier is unavailable.

For an existing site, confirm how useful content and URLs will be handled. A rebuild should have a reason beyond changing the technology. Start with the [website enquiry checks](/blog/website-not-generating-enquiries/) to identify the actual gap.

## Ask to see the working journey

Review examples on your phone. Find a service, understand the offer and try the relevant contact or booking flow. Ask the supplier to explain the decisions behind it and how the business maintains the site.
